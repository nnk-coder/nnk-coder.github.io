Three problems worth describing in detail

Carbon projects rarely fail on intent. They fail on the gap between what a methodology requires and what a field team can actually record.

Earthbanc
2022 — 2026

Half the saplings were dying. The cause was not horticultural.

Operational turnaround — $4M of programme value recovered

The problem

Sapling mortality across a five-partner, 1,000-user delivery network was running at roughly double what the programme could absorb. The assumption was that this was a planting-quality problem, which would have meant buying the loss down with more saplings.

What I did

Ran structured discovery and root-cause analysis instead of accepting the horticultural framing. The failure traced to workflow and incentive design — what field teams were asked to record, when, and what they were paid for. Rebuilt the SOPs and reporting cadence around that finding, and redesigned the product to make the right action the easy one.

Outcome

Sapling loss cut by roughly half. Around $4M of programme value recovered — without a corresponding increase in planting spend.

Prototypes

Things I built to test whether an idea holds

Four working prototypes, each taking a problem that is normally answered by an expensive site visit and asking whether open data and a model can answer it first. All are live — open them and press the buttons.

01
VM0047

ARR Feasibility Analysis — VM0047 eligibility screening for India

The problem

Land only qualifies for ARR under VM0047 if it has been non-forest for the full lookback period. Developers routinely discover a parcel is ineligible after spending months on it, because checking properly means stitching together several satellite datasets that disagree with each other.

The solution

Upload parcels as KML, KMZ or GeoJSON, or draw them on the map, and get eligible area back in minutes. Three datasets rather than one: current land cover catches natural regrowth, Hansen's loss-year band catches land that was forest until recently, and the 2000 canopy baseline catches the rest. Forest definition is configurable — the India FSI/UNFCCC default is 10% canopy, 1.0 ha minimum, 2 m height. Results come back in three classes, not two, because land that is merely flagged — wetland, steep slope, protected-area overlap — is often recoverable with documentation and should not be silently discarded.

02
Asset diligence

A solar farm's paperwork says one thing. What did the satellites see?

The problem

Before anyone lends against a solar asset, buys it, insures it or audits it, there is a reported record — a capacity, a build year, an owner. Almost none of it has been checked, and checking it has meant sending an engineer to each site.

The solution

Five of India's largest solar plants, 2,628 MW in total, tested against nine years of free satellite archives — Sentinel-2, Dynamic World, ERA5-Land, JRC Global Surface Water and Copernicus DEM. Five checks per asset produce a weighted risk score and a written argument you can disagree with. Every threshold is published, and where the evidence runs out the page says so instead of guessing: the construction check could not be resolved for any asset in the run, so every score is reported as a lower bound. It does not replace a site visit — it decides which sites are worth one.

03
Machine learning

Detecting saplings before they have a crown to see

The problem

A young sapling has no canopy signature in RGB imagery, and at 10–30 m per pixel it occupies under 1% of a pixel — so pixel-level classification misses it entirely. For ARR monitoring that is the exact period you most need to measure, because it is when mortality happens.

The solution

Use biochemistry instead of shape. Woody tissue absorbs at wavelengths grass does not, so 200 hyperspectral bands across 400–2500 nm resolve what RGB cannot — the lignin index around 1754 nm is the strongest signal. The pipeline computes five spectral indices, runs NNLS spectral unmixing to flag pixels containing as little as 20% sapling, and compares an SVM baseline against a 1D-CNN on the full band stack. A proof of concept on the Indian Pines AVIRIS scene, with a four-phase route to production dMRV and VM0047 integration.

04
Public safety

Four bathing sites, one pre-dawn hour. How many people can actually stand there?

The problem

The Nashik–Trimbakeshwar Simhastha expects roughly 12 crore visitors, about four times the 2015 event, concentrated onto four Amrit Snan mornings. Crowd collapse at a Kumbh is not a freak event — it has a mechanism that repeats: more people are fed toward a point than the ground there can clear, and then something closes.

The solution

Measure the open ground at each site from public map data, work out what it can process in the busiest hour, and say where the people it cannot process are supposed to go. Then run leave-one-out on the route network to price a single closure, and check toilets and drinking water against national norms. The finding is uncomfortable and specific: 1,185,728 people with nowhere to go, four sites with no redundancy, 60,206 toilet seats required against 24 mapped. The page is equally explicit that four of six failure modes are foreseeable years ahead and two are not, because widening an approach route has a lead time of years, not hours.
